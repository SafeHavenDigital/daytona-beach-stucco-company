## Voice Summary

The contractor you actually want to show up: calm, plainly expert, and
unbothered by a wall that looks like a disaster to you. Talks about stucco the
way a good doctor talks about a diagnosis — names the problem, explains what
caused it, tells you what it costs and how long it takes, and doesn't
dramatize it to close the sale. Confident enough to say "that's not as bad as
it looks" when it isn't, and honest enough to say "that's worse than you
think" when it is.

The feeling on the other end should be relief. The homeowner has been staring
at a crack and imagining the worst number. This voice replaces a vague fear
with a specific plan.

## Core Personality Traits

- **Calm authority.** Never matches the customer's panic. A cracked wall is
  routine here — it's the customer's first stucco problem, it's not ours.
  Steady, evenly-paced, no exclamation points. The reassurance comes from
  competence, not from cheerleading.

- **Plain-spoken expertise.** Knows the technical reality cold — hairline vs.
  structural cracking, moisture intrusion behind the lath, the difference
  between a patch and a proper re-stucco — and translates it into words a
  homeowner understands without ever sounding like the homeowner is being
  talked down to. Explains the *why* behind the recommendation, always.

- **Straight about money and time.** Gives ranges before being asked. Says
  what makes a job cost more. Says when a repair is a waste of money and a
  replacement is the honest answer, and says when the opposite is true. Will
  actively talk a customer out of work they don't need — which is the exact
  behavior that earns the bigger job later.

- **Local and rooted.** Daytona Beach, Ormond, Port Orange, New Smyrna,
  Deltona, Palm Coast. Knows what salt air does to a west-facing wall three
  blocks off A1A and what a hurricane season does to an unsealed control
  joint. Speaks about this coast as home, not as a service territory.

- **Proof over adjectives.** Doesn't claim "quality craftsmanship." Shows the
  job, names the neighborhood, states the license number, lets the photo do
  the work.

## Vocabulary

**Words and phrases to USE:**

- "Here's what's actually happening" — the signature diagnostic transition.
  Moves from the customer's symptom to the real cause.
- "Most of the time…" / "In about nine out of ten of these…" — honest
  frequency framing. Sets expectation without over-promising.
- "That's usually a {X}-day job" — time specificity, offered unprompted.
- "Somewhere in the {LOW}–{HIGH} range, depending on…" — price honesty with
  the variable named right after.
- "You don't need…" — the trust move. Ruling work *out* is the strongest
  credibility signal a contractor has.
- "Hairline," "structural," "moisture intrusion," "control joint," "lath,"
  "delamination" — real terms, each explained the first time in-page.
- "Salt air," "hurricane season," "after the storm," "west-facing wall" —
  coastal context that proves local knowledge.
- "Licensed and insured — {LICENSE_NUMBER}" — stated plainly, never as a badge
  of honor.
- "I'll take a look and tell you straight." — signature close.
- "Send me a photo." — the lowest-friction first ask, used constantly.
- Neighborhood names: Daytona Beach, Ormond Beach, Port Orange, New Smyrna
  Beach, Holly Hill, South Daytona, Deltona, Palm Coast, Ponce Inlet.

**Words and phrases to AVOID:**

- "Quality craftsmanship," "attention to detail," "customer satisfaction is
  our top priority" — the four horsemen of contractor copy. Every competitor
  says them, so they carry zero information.
- "Free estimate!" with an exclamation point, or as the headline. The estimate
  being free is table stakes, not an offer.
- "ACT NOW," "LIMITED TIME," countdown urgency — fake scarcity destroys trust
  in a trade where the customer already fears being pressured.
- "Don't wait — this will only get worse!" as a fear close. State the actual
  consequence and timeline instead ("water behind stucco doesn't dry out on
  its own; a small repair now is usually {LOW}–{HIGH}, a wall rebuild later
  isn't").
- "We're passionate about stucco." Nobody is, and nobody believes it.
- "Premier," "leading," "trusted," "#1," "award-winning" — unearned authority
  words. If there's a real award, name the award.
- "State-of-the-art," "cutting-edge," "innovative solutions" — nothing about
  this trade is cutting-edge, and claiming it reads as a lie.
- "Every job is different, so we can't give a price." Category-standard
  evasion. The whole strategic point is refusing to say this.
- "Reach out," "circle back," "at your earliest convenience" — office-speak in
  a trade voice.
- AI tells: "delve," "elevate," "unlock," "seamless," "robust,"
  "comprehensive," "in today's world," "when it comes to," "look no further."
- Em-dash-heavy rhythm and tricolon lists ("fast, reliable, affordable"). Both
  read as generated.

**Jargon level:** Light and always translated. Use the real term, then define
it in the same breath — "delamination, which just means the stucco has come
loose from the wall behind it." Teaching the customer the word is itself a
trust signal; using it without explaining is not.

**Profanity:** Never.

## Rhythm & Structure

**Sentences:** Short to medium. 8-16 words typical. A short sentence lands the
point after a longer explanatory one. Never more than two long sentences in a
row. Fragments are fine for emphasis, used sparingly. Read it out loud — if it
doesn't sound like something a person would say standing in a driveway, cut it.

**Paragraphs:** 1-3 sentences. Heavy white space. Mobile-first — a large share
of this traffic is a homeowner standing outside looking at the wall on their
phone. Nothing should be a wall of text.

**Openings:** Start with the customer's symptom or question, stated in their
words, then pivot to the cause. "You've got cracks running up from the corner
of a window. Here's what that usually means." Never open with a company
introduction. Never open with "In today's market."

**Closings:** One clear next action, low-friction, stated once and calmly.
"Send me a photo and I'll tell you what you're looking at." Not stacked CTAs,
not repeated urgency.

**Formatting:** Headers that ask the customer's actual question ("How much
does stucco repair cost in Daytona Beach?"). Short bulleted lists for options,
costs, and steps. Bold for numbers and the single most important sentence in a
section. No emoji anywhere. No exclamation points except in genuine thanks
("Thanks!").

**Numbers:** Always specific, always sourced from reality. A named range
beats a vague adjective every time. If the real number isn't known yet, the
placeholder stays until someone supplies it.

## POV & Address

**First person:** "I" for the owner's voice — estimates, opinions,
recommendations, follow-up emails. "We" for crew capacity and process ("we'll
be on site two to three days"). Mixing them deliberately is correct: the
judgment is one person's, the work is a team's.

**Reader address:** "You," direct and singular. Never "homeowners" in the third
person when speaking to one.

**Relationship stance:** The trusted tradesman. Not a peer, not a guru, not a
salesperson. The stance is a skilled professional who has seen this exact
problem several hundred times and is telling you what he'd do if it were his
house. Authority comes from repetition and specificity, never from status
claims.

## Conversion Behaviors (goal: revenue / quote conversion)

The voice carries specific jobs given the stated goal. These are voice rules,
not tactics, because how they're said determines whether they work.

- **Answer the price question before it's asked.** Every service page states a
  range and names the two or three variables that move it. This is the primary
  differentiator and the highest-leverage voice decision in the profile.
- **Lower the first ask.** The conversion event is not "book an inspection,"
  it's "send a photo." Photo-first framing removes the fear of a salesperson
  in the driveway and produces a qualified lead with a visual already attached.
- **Name the distress state out loud.** "Closing in three weeks and the
  inspector flagged the stucco?" — recognizing the specific situation converts
  far better than generic service description. Core states to write to:
  pre-sale repair deadline, failed inspection, insurance claim, HOA violation
  letter, post-storm damage, visible cracking that's been ignored for years.
- **Front-load proof.** License number, {YEARS_IN_BUSINESS}, {REVIEW_COUNT},
  and a real local photo appear above the fold, stated flatly. Trust in this
  category is the whole sale.
- **Rule things out.** At least one place per page where work is ruled
  unnecessary. "If the crack is thinner than a credit card edge and it's not
  spreading, you probably don't need me yet." This costs a few small jobs and
  wins the large ones.
- **Follow-up voice is helpful, not chasing.** Sequence emails deliver
  something useful each time — what the crack type means, what to expect on
  inspection day, what insurance typically covers. Never "just checking in,"
  never "did you see my last email." The pressure is always zero and the
  usefulness is always non-zero.
- **Handle the ghost honestly.** A quote goes cold most often over price or
  over shopping around. Say so directly: "If the number was higher than you
  expected, tell me — sometimes there's a smaller version of this job that
  buys you a few years."

## Example Phrases

**On-brand:**

- "Those stair-step cracks above your window are the most common thing I see
  in Daytona. Nine times out of ten it's settling, not structural — but I'd
  want to check for moisture behind it before I told you that for certain."
- "Most stucco repairs on a house this size run {LOW} to {HIGH}. What moves
  that number is how much of the wall has to come off and whether there's
  water damage underneath."
- "Honestly? Patching that is throwing money at it. The wall's got two more
  years of this before you're doing it again. Here's what I'd do instead."
- "Send me a photo of the crack with something for scale next to it — a coin
  works. I can usually tell you what you're dealing with from the picture."
- "Salt air out here is hard on stucco. Three blocks from the water, a west
  wall takes a beating that the same house inland never would."
- "You don't need a full re-stucco. You need about forty feet of it fixed
  right."
- "Licensed and insured, {LICENSE_NUMBER}. {YEARS_IN_BUSINESS} years doing
  this in Volusia County."
- "If you're closing soon and the inspector flagged it, tell me the date. I'll
  tell you straight whether I can get it done in time."

**Off-brand:**

- "We are the premier stucco contractor in Central Florida, dedicated to
  quality craftsmanship and total customer satisfaction!" — Says nothing, three
  banned phrases, one exclamation point too many.
- "Don't let stucco damage destroy your home's value! ACT NOW before it's too
  late!!" — Manufactured fear, hype punctuation, and the exact pressure this
  customer is afraid of.
- "Every project is unique, so pricing varies. Contact us for a free estimate
  today!" — The category-standard price dodge. This is the specific thing the
  brand refuses to do.
- "Our team of experienced professionals leverages cutting-edge techniques to
  deliver seamless exterior solutions." — Corporate AI mush. No human says
  "leverages" about stucco.
- "We're passionate about transforming homes and exceeding expectations." —
  Unbelievable and empty.
- "When it comes to stucco repair, look no further." — Two AI tells stacked
  in nine words.

## Do's and Don'ts

**DO:**

- Give a real price range on every service page, with the variables named.
- Explain the cause before recommending the fix. Diagnosis earns the sale.
- Use "send me a photo" as the primary low-friction conversion ask.
- Name specific neighborhoods, streets, and coastal conditions.
- Say when work isn't needed, or when a cheaper option is the honest one.
- State the license number, years, and review count plainly and early —
  once the real values exist.
- Keep paragraphs to 1-3 sentences. Assume a phone screen in the sun.
- Write follow-ups that teach something, so they're worth opening.
- Match the customer's actual situation: inspection, insurance, HOA, sale,
  storm.

**DON'T:**

- Use urgency the customer's situation didn't create.
- Claim superlatives, awards, or rankings without the specific proof.
- Dodge the cost question or hide behind "every job is different."
- Use exclamation points, emoji, or all-caps.
- Deploy the banned adjective set: quality, premier, trusted, passionate,
  comprehensive, seamless, cutting-edge.
- Ship copy containing an unfilled `{PLACEHOLDER}` — get the number or cut
  the claim. Never invent one.
- Talk down to the homeowner, or use a technical term without translating it.
- Send a "just checking in" follow-up.
- Panic alongside the customer. Calm is the product.
