# The Stucco Triage Guide

### What your stucco crack is actually telling you

---

You noticed a crack. Now you are standing in your yard trying to work out
whether you are looking at something cosmetic or something expensive.

That is a hard question to answer from where you are standing, and the internet
is not much help — most of what you will find is either a maintenance checklist
written for walls that are fine, or a contractor telling you to call
immediately.

So here is the honest version.

Most stucco cracks in Volusia County are not emergencies. A good number of them
genuinely do not need anything done this year. Some do. The difference is
usually readable from the outside, if you know what you are looking at — and
this guide is how you read it.

There is also a category this guide cannot resolve, and we will be straight
about which one that is when we get there.

**What you need:** a tape measure or ruler with millimetre or 1/16-inch
markings, and about ten minutes.

---

## First: the one measurement that matters most

Before anything else, measure the width of the crack at its widest point.

Width is the single most useful number you can collect, because it separates
the two broad categories of stucco cracking more reliably than length,
location, or how alarming it looks.

A useful reference point: **a standard credit card is about 0.76 mm thick**, and
**a US dime is about 1.35 mm.** If you can slide a credit card edge into the
crack, you are above roughly 0.8 mm. If a dime edge fits, you are above roughly
1.3 mm.

Write the number down. You will want it later, and you will want it far more in
six months.

---

## The four things you are looking at

Nearly every stucco crack a homeowner notices falls into one of four patterns.
Find yours below.

---

### Pattern 1 — Fine surface cracking, spread over an area

**What it looks like:** A web or map of very fine lines across a section of
wall. Individual lines are hairline — thinner than a credit card edge. They do
not run in one clear direction; they spread.

**What it usually is:** Shrinkage in the stucco itself as it cured, or the
surface aging. This is the most common thing homeowners call about and the
least likely to indicate a structural problem.

**Verdict: WATCH IT.**

This does not need emergency attention. It is worth keeping an eye on, worth
addressing eventually for appearance and to keep water out of the surface, and
not worth losing a weekend over.

**What would change the verdict:** If any individual line in the web widens past
a credit card edge, or if staining appears along it, re-read Pattern 3 and 4.

---

### Pattern 2 — A single straight crack running from a window or door corner

**What it looks like:** One crack, running diagonally out and up — or out and
down — from the corner of an opening. Often at roughly 45 degrees.

**What it usually is:** Stress concentration. Corners of openings are where a
wall is weakest, and normal building movement — settling, thermal expansion,
wind load — shows up there first. Extremely common, and by itself not proof of
anything serious.

**Verdict: WATCH IT — but measure it, and measure it again.**

This one is about *change over time*, not the crack itself. A stable
corner crack that has looked the same for four years is a very different thing
from one that has doubled since spring.

**Why this pattern gets its own attention here:** window and door corners are
also where water most commonly gets in, because that is where flashing and
sealant do the hard work. The crack may be harmless. The *junction* it is
sitting on is the highest-risk detail on the wall. If this crack is
accompanied by anything in Pattern 4, treat Pattern 4 as the answer.

---

### Pattern 3 — A wide, long, or stepped crack

**What it looks like:** Any of these — a crack wider than a dime edge; a crack
running more than a few feet in a continuous line; a crack that steps in a
staircase pattern; a crack where the two sides are no longer flush, so you can
feel a lip with your fingertip; or the same crack appearing in the same place
inside the house.

**What it usually is:** Movement that is larger than surface shrinkage. This can
mean foundation or framing movement, and it is the category most likely to be
about the structure rather than the finish.

**Verdict: GET IT LOOKED AT.**

Not a five-alarm emergency in most cases, but this is past the point where a
homeowner can reasonably assess it alone, and past the point where patching the
visible line accomplishes anything.

**Important:** if the underlying cause is structural movement, moisture testing
is not the right first call — this may need a structural engineer, and a good
contractor will tell you that rather than sell you something.

---

### Pattern 4 — Cracking with staining, softness, or bubbling

**What it looks like:** Any crack — including a hairline one — accompanied by
one or more of:

- Dark streaking, discoloration, or a tide-line stain on the wall
- Paint bubbling, blistering, or peeling near the crack
- A patch of wall that sounds hollow when tapped, or gives slightly under
  firm thumb pressure
- Efflorescence — a white, chalky, crystalline deposit
- A musty smell inside against that same wall
- Any of the above appearing below a window, along a roofline, or near a
  hose bib or downspout

**What it usually means:** Water has gotten in, or is getting in. The crack may
be the entry point, or it may be a symptom of what the water has already done
to what is behind it.

**Verdict: ACT ON THIS ONE.**

This is the pattern to take seriously, and it is the one where the width of the
crack matters least. A hairline crack with a stain under it is a more urgent
finding than a wide, clean, dry crack.

**Why:** stucco is a reservoir cladding. It is expected to get wet and then dry
out. The system fails when water gets in and *cannot* leave — and by the time
that shows on the outside, it has typically been going on for a while. The
damage is not the crack. The damage is what the water has been doing to the
lath, the sheathing, and the framing behind it.

---

## The honest limit of this guide

Here is what a guide cannot do, and it matters more than anything above.

**Everything in Pattern 4 is an outside symptom of an inside problem — and the
outside is not where the answer is.**

You can have significant moisture behind a wall that looks completely fine from
the yard. You can also have alarming-looking staining that turns out to be a
surface issue and nothing more. Both happen. Neither can be sorted out by
looking harder.

The only way to know whether there is water in a wall is to measure the moisture
in that wall. That means a meter against the wall, in the specific areas where
the readings will mean something.

That is a real limit, not a sales setup. If you have Pattern 1 or Pattern 2 with
no staining, you do not need anyone to measure anything right now. Genuinely.
Take the photo, write down the width, look again in six months.

---

## Do these three things right now

Whatever pattern you landed on, these take ten minutes and are worth doing
today.

1. **Photograph it with something for scale.** Put a coin or a tape measure next
   to the crack and take the photo straight on, not at an angle. Note where on
   the house it is.
2. **Write down the width at the widest point, and the date.** This single
   number is the most useful thing you will have in six months. A crack that has
   not moved is a fundamentally different problem from one that has.
3. **Check the ground below it after the next hard rain.** Look for where water
   actually goes — splashback, a downspout dumping against the wall, a hose bib
   dripping, grade sloping toward the house. A surprising number of stucco
   moisture problems are a drainage problem wearing a stucco costume, and that
   is the cheapest possible fix.
