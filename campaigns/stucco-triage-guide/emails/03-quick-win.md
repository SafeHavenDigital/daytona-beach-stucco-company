for a brand built on not dramatizing.

## Email Copy

Most people picture stucco as a waterproof shell. It isn't, and that
misunderstanding is behind a lot of bad repair decisions.

Stucco is a **reservoir cladding**. It's designed to absorb water during a
rain, hold it briefly, and then release it as it dries. Getting wet is normal.
Getting wet is the design.

The system fails when water gets in and *can't get back out.*

Here's roughly what's behind the finish on a typical Florida stucco wall:

- The stucco itself, in layers
- A metal or fiberglass **lath** the stucco is keyed into
- A **weather-resistive barrier** — building paper or house wrap
- **Sheathing**, then the framing
- **Flashing** at every opening, and a **weep screed** along the bottom edge

That last one matters more than almost anything else. The weep screed is a
metal strip at the base of the wall with small openings in it, and its entire
job is to let water that got in **drain back out.**

Now the useful part. When water gets in and can't leave, the damage doesn't
start at the stucco. It starts at the paper, the sheathing, and the framing —
**behind** the finish. Which means the wall can be doing real damage to itself
while the outside still looks completely fine.

That's why the triage guide stops where it does. It's not modesty. The outside
of a wall is genuinely limited evidence about the inside of a wall.

**Something you can do this weekend, and it's free:**

After the next hard rain, walk the perimeter and look at the *bottom* of your
walls. Check that the weep screed isn't buried under soil, mulch, or a poured
patio — a buried weep screed means water that gets in has nowhere to go.
Then look for where water actually lands: splashback off concrete, a downspout
dumping against the wall, a dripping hose bib, grade sloping toward the house.

A real share of stucco moisture problems are a **drainage problem in a stucco
costume.** That's the cheapest possible finding, and you can spot it yourself.
